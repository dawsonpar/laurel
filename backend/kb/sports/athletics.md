# Athletics

## Type
Olympic

## Parity Pair
para-athletics

## Rules

Athletics, or track and field, splits into track events on the oval and field events in throwing circles, sectors, and jumping pits. Track events include sprints (100m, 200m, 400m), middle distance (800m, 1500m), distance (5000m, 10000m, marathon), hurdles (110m/100m hurdles, 400m hurdles, 3000m steeplechase), and relays (4x100m, 4x400m, mixed 4x400m). Sprints up to 400m and the first turn of the 800m run in assigned lanes; longer races allow free lane choice once runners break for the inside.

Field events split into jumps (high jump, pole vault, long jump, triple jump) and throws (shot put, discus, hammer, javelin). Jumpers and throwers typically get three attempts in qualifying with three more in the final. High jump and pole vault work differently: athletes choose a starting height and continue until they record three consecutive misses at a single bar. Best mark wins, with countback for tiebreakers (fewest misses at the winning height, then fewest total misses).

False start rules changed in 2010. Before then, the field was collectively warned after the first false start, and any subsequent false start drew a DQ. Under the current rule from the international governing body for athletics (in force since the 2010 season), any single false start by an individual in a non-combined event triggers immediate disqualification. Starting blocks contain pressure sensors, and any reaction faster than 0.10 seconds after the gun is treated as a false start because science suggests humans cannot react that quickly. Combined events (decathlon, heptathlon) still use the older one-warning system.

Lane violations apply mainly to events run fully in lanes. For years, stepping on or over the inside line meant automatic DQ. As of November 2021, the rule softened: a single step on the line is permitted, but a full step over it, or any second infringement, is a DQ. The same applies if an athlete runs outside their lane on a curve or impedes another runner.

Relay rules center on the exchange zone (also called the takeover zone). As of June 2018, the international governing body for athletics merged the older 10m acceleration zone and 20m passing zone into a single 30m exchange zone for the 4x100m and other short relays. The baton, not the runner's body, defines the exchange. The pass must complete entirely within the 30m zone, or the team is DQ'd. Drops are recoverable only if the dropping runner picks the baton up themselves. In hurdle events, knocking down a hurdle with the foot or leg is generally not a DQ by itself. What does draw the flag is trailing a foot or leg below the horizontal plane of the top of the hurdle while passing beside it, or deliberately knocking a hurdle down with the hand.

## Scoring

Most events are scored objectively by time, distance, or height. Track times are recorded to the hundredth of a second for races up to 10,000m and to the second for the marathon. Photo finish cameras shoot thousands of frames per second along the finish line plane and resolve ties to the thousandth, though only hundredths appear in official results. When two athletes tie to the hundredth, the thousandths determine placing.

Horizontal field events (long jump, triple jump, throws) are measured from the take-off line or the inside front edge of the throwing circle to the nearest mark made by the athlete's body or implement. Heights (high jump, pole vault) are measured to the bar. Wind is measured for sprints up to 200m, all hurdles up to 110m/100m, and horizontal jumps; a tailwind above 2.0 m/s invalidates a performance for record purposes, though it still counts for placement.

Combined events use the scoring tables from the international governing body for athletics, dating to 1985. Decathlon (men, ten events over two days) and heptathlon (women, seven events over two days) convert each performance into points via a formula with three constants per event: Points = A x (B - P)^C for track (faster is more), inverted to A x (P - B)^C for jumps and throws. Constants are calibrated so a world-record-level performance produces around 1000 to 1100 points, balancing throwers against runners.

Disqualification triggers vary by event and drive most controversy in the sport. In sprints: a single false start (since 2010), running outside the assigned lane, fully crossing the inside line, impeding another runner, or in relays, exchanging the baton outside the 30m zone. In hurdles, the dominant trigger is trailing a leg below the plane of the hurdle around its side. In distance races, runners can be DQ'd for cutting inside the rail, pushing or impeding rivals, or in the steeplechase, going around the water jump or barriers. In throws, a foul is called when the athlete steps over the front of the circle or take-off board, when the implement lands outside the legal sector, or when the athlete leaves the circle before the implement lands. In the javelin, the implement must land tip-first to count. In long jump and triple jump, any contact with the plasticine strip beyond the take-off board is a foul.

## Glossary

- **False start**: reaction under 0.10 seconds, or any movement out of the blocks before the gun. Single false start equals DQ.
- **Reaction time**: gap between gun firing and athlete applying force to the blocks, measured by pressure sensors.
- **Lane violation**: stepping on or over the inside line, or running outside the assigned lane. One step on the line is permitted; over the line, or a second offense, is a DQ.
- **Exchange zone**: the 30m section of track where a relay baton must be passed.
- **Takeover zone**: same as exchange zone.
- **Fly time**: unofficial time for one segment of a relay leg, used by coaches to evaluate handoff quality.
- **Photo finish**: high-speed image of the finish line, separating close finishes to the thousandth of a second.
- **Wind-aided**: performance with a tailwind above 2.0 m/s; counts for the meet but not for records.
- **Anchor leg**: the final runner in a relay.
- **Second leg**: runner who receives the baton from the lead-off and hands to the third leg.
- **Blocks**: metal foot pedals sprinters push out of at the start. Required for races up to 400m.
- **Bell lap**: the final lap of a distance race, signaled by a bell.
- **Kick**: closing acceleration of a distance runner, usually the last 200 to 400 meters.
- **Splits**: intermediate times recorded at fixed distances.
- **Spikes**: metal pins on the bottom of track shoes that grip the surface.
- **Scratch**: athlete who withdraws from an event before competing.
- **Foul**: invalid attempt in a field event, signaled by a red flag.
- **Fair**: valid attempt, signaled by a white flag.
- **Throwing circle**: round concrete pad inside which shot, discus, and hammer throwers stand. Stepping over the front rim is a foul.
- **Sector**: wedge-shaped legal landing area for throws.
- **Javelin**: spear-shaped implement that must land tip-first to count.
- **Shot put**: metal ball that must be pushed (not thrown) from the neck.
- **Discus**: flat weighted plate thrown from a circle with a spinning motion.
- **Hammer**: metal ball on a wire and handle, thrown after multiple rotations.
- **Pole vault**: athletes plant a flexible pole and vault over a horizontal bar.
- **High jump**: athletes clear a horizontal bar with no aid, typically using the Fosbury flop.
- **Long jump**: single horizontal jump from a take-off board into a sand pit.
- **Triple jump**: hop, step, and jump sequence into a sand pit.
- **Decathlon**: ten-event combined competition for men over two days.
- **Heptathlon**: seven-event combined competition for women over two days.
- **Hurdle**: barrier set at fixed heights and spacings; athletes clear them while sprinting.
- **Trail leg**: rear leg as a hurdler clears a barrier. Must stay above the plane of the hurdle top.

## Notable Records & Historical Context

Athletics is the world records sport. Times and distances are objective, events are stable across decades, and a record-eligible performance carries enormous weight. Current world records (verified as of April 2026):

1. **Men's 100m**: 9.58 seconds, Berlin, August 16, 2009. Set at the World Championships, taking 0.11 off the prior holder's Beijing 2008 mark. Top measured speed of 12.32 m/s.
2. **Men's 200m**: 19.19 seconds, Berlin, August 20, 2009. Set four days after the 100m record at the same Championships by the same Jamaican sprinter.
3. **Men's marathon**: 2:00:35, Chicago, October 8, 2023. The first record-eligible sub-2:01 marathon, taking 34 seconds off the prior mark. The Kenyan record holder and his coach died in a road accident in Kenya on February 11, 2024, days after the record was ratified.
4. **Women's 100m**: 10.49 seconds, Indianapolis, July 16, 1988, in the quarterfinals of the US Olympic Trials. The wind reading was zero, though some statisticians have questioned whether the gauge was functioning correctly. Recognized by the international governing body for athletics.
5. **Women's marathon**: 2:09:56, Chicago, October 13, 2024. The first sub-2:11 and sub-2:10 women's marathon, breaking the prior Ethiopian holder's 2:11:53 by nearly two minutes.
6. **Men's high jump**: 2.45 meters, Salamanca, July 27, 1993. The Cuban record holder remains the only person to clear 8 feet. The Qatari high jumper's 2.43m in 2014 is the closest anyone has come.
7. **Men's pole vault**: 6.31 meters, Uppsala, March 2025. The Swedish record holder has broken the pole vault world record 15 times as of the March 2025 mark, often by a single centimeter, including 6.30m for gold at the 2025 World Championships in Tokyo before pushing it to 6.31m.
8. **Men's 400m hurdles**: 45.94 seconds, at the Olympic Games Tokyo 2020 (held August 3, 2021). The Norwegian record holder took roughly three quarters of a second off his own record. The American silver medalist ran 46.17, also under the old world record.
9. **Women's 400m hurdles**: 50.37 seconds, Paris, August 8, 2024, by the American defending Olympic champion. She also ran 50.65 at the 2024 US Olympic Trials in June and 51.46 winning gold at the Olympic Games Tokyo 2020.
10. **Men's long jump**: 8.95 meters, Tokyo, August 30, 1991, at the World Championships. The American record holder beat the prior nearly 23-year-old record by 5cm in a competition where another American produced three of the longest legal jumps ever but finished second.
11. **Women's long jump**: 7.52 meters, Leningrad, June 11, 1988. The longest-standing women's long jump record, set by a Soviet jumper.
12. **Men's javelin**: 98.48 meters, Jena, May 25, 1996. The Czech thrower set the record in the third round; it has stood for nearly 30 years.
13. **Men's shot put**: 23.56 meters, Los Angeles, May 27, 2023, at the USATF LA Grand Prix. The American record holder added 19cm to his own mark using a new technique with a longer step across the ring.
14. **Men's discus**: 75.56 meters, April 13, 2025. The Lithuanian record holder broke his own record twice on the same day (74.89, then 75.56), surpassing the prior German holder's mark from 1986.
15. **Men's hammer throw**: 86.74 meters, Stuttgart, August 30, 1986. One of the oldest standing world records in athletics, held by a Soviet thrower.

Famous DQ moments useful for the demo:

- **2011 World Championships, Daegu**: the defending world 100m champion false-started in the men's 100m final and was disqualified under the then-new (2010) zero-tolerance rule. His Jamaican training partner won gold in his absence.
- **USA men's 4x100m relay**: the US has been disqualified roughly 11 times since 1988, mostly for exchanges outside the zone. At the Olympic Games Paris 2024, the second-leg runner left too early and had to stop to receive from the lead-off, completing the pass outside the zone.
- **USA mixed 4x400m relay, Olympic Games Tokyo 2020**: initially disqualified for an exchange zone infringement, then reinstated on appeal, illustrating that DQs can be overturned by officials reviewing video.
- **2020 World Indoors, Birmingham**: 21 lane-violation disqualifications at a single championship, prompting the 2021 rule change that allows one step on the inside line.

## What to Look For

A sprint finish is decided at the line where the torso (not the head, arms, or legs) crosses. If a race looks tied to the eye, the photo finish image resolves it. The wind reading appears on the scoreboard within seconds; a number above +2.0 m/s next to a fast time means the result will not stand for record or qualifying purposes, even though athletes still get the placement and prize money.

To spot a false start, watch the start in slow motion: any movement of the shoulders, hips, or feet before the gun is visible. The official call comes from block sensor data, not the eye. A starter's recall (a second gunshot or whistle) signals a false start and the field re-sets.

Lane violations are easiest to spot on the curve in the 200m and 400m. Watch the inside foot relative to the white line. Stepping on the line is now allowed once; stepping fully over it, or doing it twice, is a DQ. In the relay exchange zone, two yellow triangular markers on the track edge define the start and end of the 30m zone. The baton, not the runner, must be inside the zone while it changes hands. Watch the baton, not the feet.

In throws, a fair attempt gets a white flag; a foul gets red. For shot, discus, and hammer, the foul is usually a foot touching the top of the circle's iron rim, or the athlete leaving the circle from the front. For javelin, watch the tip on landing: it must mark the ground tip-first. For long jump and triple jump, the take-off board has a plasticine strip; any imprint is a foul. The jump is measured from the front edge of the board, so a jumper who takes off well behind it loses distance.

Photo finish images are vertical strips assembled from the line over time, which is why athletes can look stretched or compressed in them. The athlete whose torso crosses first wins, regardless of body position.
