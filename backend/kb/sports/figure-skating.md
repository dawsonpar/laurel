# Figure Skating

## Type
Olympic

## Parity Pair
None

## Rules
Olympic figure skating has four individual disciplines (men's singles, women's singles, pairs, ice dance) plus a team event. Pairs add throws and overhead lifts. Ice dance has no throws or side-by-side jumps and centers on synchronized footwork.

Singles and pairs each run two segments: a short program (about 2:40, with a fixed list of required elements) and a free skate (about 4:00, longer and less restricted). Total score is the sum. Ice dance uses a rhythm dance (about 2:50) and a free dance (about 4:00).

A singles free skate currently includes up to seven jump elements (one must be an Axel-type), three spins, one step sequence, and one choreographic sequence. Pairs add throw jumps, twist lifts, lifts, and death spirals. Starting in 2026 to 2027 the ISU is reducing singles to six jumps and adjusting spin and lift requirements.

Two panels evaluate each performance. The technical panel (a controller plus two specialists) identifies what was performed, calls levels, and flags errors like under-rotations or wrong edges. The judging panel, up to nine judges plus a referee, scores quality and artistic components. Penalties cover time, costume malfunctions, falls, illegal elements, and music violations. Outright disqualification is rare and usually tied to anti-doping or eligibility issues rather than judging calls.

## Scoring
The ISU Judging System (IJS) replaced the old 6.0 system after the 2002 Salt Lake City scandal. Total score is Technical Element Score (TES) plus Program Component Score (PCS), minus deductions.

TES is element by element. Every jump, spin, lift, and step sequence has a published base value. Judges add a Grade of Execution (GOE) from minus 5 to plus 5. The panel's GOE is the trimmed mean (highest and lowest dropped, the rest averaged) and is added to base value.

PCS rewards artistry. Since the 2022 to 2023 reform, judges score three components (Composition, Presentation, Skating Skills) on a 0.25 to 10 scale, then weighted to a final PCS.

Several specific calls "negate a highlight." A jump short by a quarter to a half rotation gets an under-rotation flag ("<") and base value drops (commonly to about 70 percent). A jump short by half a rotation or more is downgraded ("<<") and scored as the next jump down (a downgraded triple Lutz scores like a double Lutz). A wrong edge on a Lutz or flip ("e") cuts base value by roughly 30 percent. An unclear edge ("!") leaves base value alone but forces a GOE cut. Falls trigger a flat 1-point deduction (more for repeated falls) on top of a heavily negative GOE. So a skater can land a stunning quad and still lose points if the panel sees a wrong edge, a quarter-short rotation, or a brief two-foot landing.

## Glossary
- Triple Axel: three and a half rotations from a forward outside edge. Hardest standard triple.
- Quad Lutz: four rotations from a back outside edge with toe-pick assist.
- Throw Jump: pairs element where the man launches the woman into a multi-rotation jump she lands alone.
- Twizzle: a one-foot traveling turn with continuous rotation, a signature ice dance element.
- GOE: Grade of Execution. Minus 5 to plus 5 quality score added to each element's base value.
- PCS: Program Component Score. Artistic score across Composition, Presentation, and Skating Skills.
- Edge Call: flag that a Lutz or flip launched from the wrong edge ("e" clear, "!" unclear).
- Under-Rotation: a jump short by a quarter to a half rotation (marked "<").
- Downgrade: a jump short by half a rotation or more ("<<"), scored as the next jump down.
- Free Skate: longer second segment in singles and pairs, also called the long program.
- Short Program: shorter first segment with a fixed list of required elements.
- Salchow: edge jump from a back inside edge, no toe-pick assist.
- Lutz: toe jump from a back outside edge, named after Alois Lutz.
- Flip: toe jump from a back inside edge.
- Loop: edge jump from a back outside edge, landed on the same edge.
- Toe Loop: most common toe jump, back outside edge with toe-pick assist. Lowest base value.
- Axel: only standard jump with a forward takeoff, adding a half rotation.
- Combination Spin: a spin that changes positions and feet without stopping.
- Step Sequence: continuous pattern of turns and steps across the ice.
- Choreographic Sequence: free-form artistic passage scored on impression, not counted elements.
- Death Spiral: pairs element where the man pivots and the woman circles low to the ice on one edge.
- Q Call: "q" flag (added 2020 to 2021) for jumps exactly a quarter short. Base value unchanged, GOE reduced.

## Notable Records & Historical Context
The ISU reset its world-record book starting in 2018 to 2019 after expanding the GOE scale, so older marks are tracked as "historical." Under the historical system, Yuzuru Hanyu of Japan set the Olympic record at PyeongChang 2018 with a 317.85 total (111.68 short, 206.17 free). Hanyu, now retired, is the only male skater since Dick Button (late 1940s to early 1950s) to win back-to-back Olympic golds (2014 and 2018).

The current men's world record on the post-2018 scale stands at 333.81, set in October 2025. The highest women's total under the post-2018 scale was 272.71 at the November 2021 Rostelecom Cup, though that mark sits inside the cluster of scores contested during the Russian doping case. [VERIFY: whether the ISU has formally annulled or kept the 272.71 women's mark as of April 2026.]

Quad firsts: Kurt Browning landed the first ratified quad toe loop in 1988, Timothy Goebel the first quad Salchow in 1998, Brandon Mroz the first quad Lutz in 2011, and Shoma Uno the first quad flip in 2016. The first woman to land a triple Axel in international competition was Midori Ito at the 1988 NHK Trophy. Ito later won the 1989 World title and 1992 Olympic silver.

The longest national dynasty belongs to the Soviet Union and Russia in pairs: from 1964 through 2006, Soviet or Russian pairs won 12 consecutive Olympic gold medals, a 42-year streak. Irina Rodnina, now retired, won three straight Olympic golds (1972, 1976, 1980) with two different partners. In ice dance, the Soviet team of Pakhomova and Gorshkov won the discipline's first Olympic gold at Innsbruck 1976.

## What to Look For
A clean landing leaves the skater gliding backward on one foot, free leg extended, arms open, no scrape, no wobble. A "save" shows a deep knee bend, a quick free-foot tap, or a hand to the ice. Those keep the skater upright but cost GOE.

To spot under-rotation, watch the body, not the ice. If the rotation is still visibly turning when the blade meets the ice, the jump came up short. A clean rotation completes in the air and tracks straight backward from touchdown. Slow-motion makes this much easier than live viewing.

Jump types are easier to tell apart by the takeoff than the air position. An Axel is the only jump that takes off skating forward. Toe jumps (toe loop, flip, Lutz) feature a clear backward "pick" with the free toe stabbing the ice. Edge jumps (Salchow, loop) launch off the blade with no pick. The Lutz uses a back outside edge, the flip an inside edge, so a skater "fighting" the curve at launch is a likely edge-call moment.

In pairs and ice dance, watch unison: free legs, head turns, and arms should mirror within a fraction of a second. In ice dance, twizzles are the signature element. In pairs, throws and lifts are the visual highlights, but the panel also weighs entry, lifting-arm extension, and exit position.
