# Para Alpine Skiing

## Type
Paralympic

## Parity Pair
None

## Rules

Para Alpine Skiing is contested at the Paralympic Winter Games across the same five disciplines as Olympic alpine racing: Downhill, Super-G, Giant Slalom, Slalom, and Super Combined (one Super-G run plus one Slalom run). Athletes race against the clock down a gated course, and the fastest factored time wins. Slalom and Giant Slalom are decided over two combined runs.

Course standards mirror able-bodied World Cup events. Vertical drops, gate spacing, and snow preparation follow the international ski federation's specifications, with adjustments made for sitting-class safety and visually impaired visibility. Athletes complete inspection runs before racing, sliding alongside the gates without skiing them to memorize line and rhythm.

Equipment varies dramatically by classification. Sitting-class skiers race on a sit-ski (also called a mono-ski), a molded bucket seat mounted on a shock-absorbing frame above a single ski, steered with two short outriggers (forearm crutches with miniature ski tips) for balance. Standing skiers may use one or two skis, one or two poles, and prosthetics or outriggers depending on their impairment. Visually impaired skiers use standard alpine equipment.

Visually impaired athletes ski with a sighted guide, and the pair is treated as a single competing unit. Guide and athlete communicate via Bluetooth helmet headsets, with the guide calling turns, terrain, and gates in real time. The guide may ski ahead of, behind, or alongside the athlete depending on class and preference, but the two cannot make physical contact. Both must complete the course, and both receive medals.

Times are not compared raw. Each finishing time is multiplied by a percentage factor assigned to the athlete's classification, producing a "factored time" that is the official scoring time. The system, in place since the Paralympic Winter Games Torino 2006, lets athletes from different classes within the same category compete in a single combined medal event. Factors are recalculated annually based on more than 400 elite races over a four-year window.

## Scoring

Scoring uses the formula Scoring Time = Raw Time x Factor. Each classification has a factor at or below 1.0, and athletes with more severe impairments receive lower factors (which shrink their raw time more, giving them an effective handicap). For example, a B1 visually impaired skier might cross the finish in 65 seconds with a factor of 0.88, producing a scoring time of 57.2 seconds, while a B3 skier crossing in 60 seconds with a factor of 0.98 scores 58.8 seconds. The B1 wins on factored time despite a slower raw time.

This is the most counterintuitive thing for new viewers. The clock you see at the finish is not the clock that decides the medal. Broadcasts now overlay the factored time directly on screen, but the factor itself is set in advance of the season, not adjusted live. Within each of the three categories, all classes race together for one set of medals. There are no separate medals for LW2 versus LW9 or for B1 versus B3.

## Classifications

Classifications are governed by the international Paralympic governing body and the world para snow sports authority. Codes use "LW" (locomotor winter) for physical impairments and "B" for vision impairments.

**STANDING (LW1 to LW9)**

- LW1: Severe impairment in both legs, such as bilateral above-knee amputation. Skis on one or two skis with two poles or outriggers.
- LW2: Severe impairment in a single leg, typically a single above-knee amputation. Skis on one ski with two outriggers.
- LW3: Moderate impairment in both legs, such as bilateral below-knee amputation or mild cerebral palsy. Skis on two skis with two poles.
- LW4: Moderate impairment in a single leg, typically a single below-knee amputation. Skis on two skis with two poles.
- LW5/7: Impairment in both arms, such as bilateral arm amputation. Skis on two skis with no poles, relying on core and leg strength for balance.
- LW6/8: Impairment in a single arm. Skis on two skis with one pole.
- LW9: Combined impairment affecting at least one arm and at least one leg, on the same or opposite sides. Equipment varies based on which limbs are most affected.

**SITTING (LW10 to LW12, mono-ski / sit-ski)**

- LW10: No functional sitting balance, typically high-level spinal cord injury with little or no abdominal or trunk muscle function. Athlete is held upright entirely by the bucket and straps.
- LW11: Fair sitting balance with partial trunk control but no usable hip function.
- LW12: Good trunk control with reduced hip and leg function, such as lower-level spinal cord injury or bilateral above-knee amputation choosing to race seated.

**VISUALLY IMPAIRED (B1 to B3, raced with a sighted guide)**

- B1: Total or near-total blindness, ranging from no light perception to inability to recognize a hand at any distance. B1 athletes must wear approved blacked-out goggles to ensure fairness.
- B2: Low vision, with visual acuity up to roughly LogMAR 1.5 and/or a visual field constricted to less than 5 degrees radius.
- B3: Moderate vision impairment, the least severe class, with visual acuity up to roughly LogMAR 1.4 and/or a visual field constricted to less than 20 degrees radius.

## Glossary

- **Sit-ski**: Generic term for the seated equipment used in the Sitting category.
- **Mono-ski**: The single-ski version of a sit-ski, with a bucket seat on a sprung frame over one ski.
- **Outrigger**: A short forearm crutch with a miniature ski tip on the bottom, used by sit-skiers for balance and by some standing skiers in place of a pole.
- **Slalom gate**: Single-pole gates set close together, requiring quick short-radius turns.
- **GS gate**: Two-pole panel gates used in Giant Slalom, set wider apart for medium-radius turns.
- **Course inspection**: Pre-race slow descent alongside the gates to memorize line and terrain.
- **DH**: Downhill, the longest and fastest event with the widest turns and biggest jumps.
- **DSQ**: Disqualified, typically for missing a gate or violating equipment rules.
- **DNF**: Did Not Finish, awarded when an athlete falls or skis off the course.
- **Time factor**: The percentage multiplier applied to raw time based on classification.
- **Factored time**: The raw finishing time after the factor has been applied. The official scoring time.
- **Guide skier**: The sighted athlete who skis with a visually impaired racer, calling turns and terrain.
- **Communication system**: Bluetooth helmet headsets that link guide and athlete during the run.
- **Tucking**: The aerodynamic crouch used on flat sections to maintain speed.
- **Line**: The trajectory chosen through a series of gates.
- **Fall line**: The most direct path gravity would pull an object down the slope. Good racers keep their skis pointed near the fall line.
- **Forerunner**: A non-competing skier who tests the course before racing begins.
- **Bib**: The numbered vest worn by each racer, indicating start order.
- **Combined**: An event scored on the sum of times from two different disciplines (Super-G plus Slalom).

## Notable Records & Historical Context

Para Alpine Skiing debuted at the first Paralympic Winter Games in Ornskoldsvik, Sweden in 1976 with Slalom and Giant Slalom for standing skiers. Downhill was added at the Paralympic Winter Games Innsbruck 1984, sit-ski racing became fully medaled in the 1990s, and Super-G joined at the Paralympic Winter Games Lillehammer 1994.

Austria is the most decorated nation in the discipline, with roughly 280 all-time Paralympic medals (around 94 golds). Austria and the United States have each topped the alpine medal table at four Paralympic Winter Games, more than any other country, with France, Germany, Norway, and Switzerland also consistent powers.

The most decorated Para alpine skier in history is a German man with 22 Paralympic medals (16 gold, 4 silver, 2 bronze) across six Games from the Paralympic Winter Games Albertville 1992 to Vancouver 2010. The most decorated woman in the sport is also German, with 19 medals. The all-sport Winter Paralympic medal record holder is a Norwegian with 27 medals (22 gold) across alpine, Nordic, and biathlon between 1988 and 2002. A French standing skier born without a left forearm swept all four standing events at the Paralympic Winter Games PyeongChang 2018.

## What to Look For

Standing skiers ski the way you would expect. Count skis and poles, and you can often guess the class on sight: one ski and two outriggers means LW2, two skis and no poles means LW5/7, two skis and one pole means LW6/8.

Sit-skiers are unmistakable. Watch the inside outrigger as they enter a turn. They drag it lightly across the snow as a balance reference, then drive hip and shoulder over the ski to carve. Because all the steering force has to come from upper-body weight transfer, sit-ski turns look more dynamic than standing turns, with the whole bucket pitching from edge to edge.

For visually impaired races, watch the spacing between guide and athlete and listen for the headset audio. A B1 athlete follows extremely close to the guide, often within a meter or two, because they are skiing on voice cues alone. B2 and B3 athletes can ski farther back and may even pass their guide on certain sections. The guide wears a brightly colored bib marked "GUIDE" so cameras can pick them out against the snow.

On the scoreboard, look for two times after each run. The first is the raw clock time. The second, usually labeled "factored" or "calc," is the official scoring time and the real medal order.
