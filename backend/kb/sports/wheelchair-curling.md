# Wheelchair Curling

## Type
Paralympic

## Parity Pair
curling

## Rules
Wheelchair curling is played over eight ends, two fewer than Olympic curling's ten. Each team fields four athletes, the team must be mixed gender, and a female athlete must be on the ice at all times. Each player throws two stones per end. At Milano Cortina 2026, a new mixed doubles event joined the mixed team event.

The biggest difference from Olympic curling is that there is no sweeping. Once a stone is released, nothing can change its path or speed. Athletes deliver from a stationary wheelchair, with the wheels in contact with the ice and feet off the ice.

Stones may be released by hand or, more commonly, with a delivery stick (also called an extender) that clips onto the stone handle so the curler can push it from a seated position. Eligibility is restricted to athletes with a lower-limb impairment significant enough to prevent a sliding delivery, including spinal cord injury, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, and double leg amputation. Wheelchair curling uses a single sport class.

The delivery looks calm but demands precise mechanics. The thrower lines up behind the hack area, often with a teammate stabilizing the chair. The curler sights the broom held by the skip and pushes the stone forward with a smooth motion. Rotation is applied by a slight twist at release.

Hammer rules carry over from Olympic curling. The team that gives up points keeps the hammer in the next end. A blanked end preserves the hammer for the team that had it.

## Scoring
Scoring is identical to Olympic curling, so the Curling KB covers the fundamentals: only one team scores per end, that team scores one point for every stone closer to the button than the opponent's nearest stone, and the team with the most points after all ends wins. A tie after eight ends goes to an extra end.

Two differences matter for viewers. Games are shorter at eight ends instead of ten, which compresses the strategic arc and makes a single big end more decisive. And because there is no sweeping, a thrower cannot rescue a stone that comes up light or drifts off line. Strategy leans toward simpler shots and conservative draws because the margin for error on weight is zero once the stone leaves the stick.

## Classifications
World Curling sets the eligibility standard. Athletes must have a lower-limb impairment that prevents a sliding delivery. Qualifying conditions include spinal cord injury, cerebral palsy, multiple sclerosis, spina bifida, polyneuropathy, and amputation or congenital limb deficiency affecting the lower limbs. There is only one sport class, and athletes pass a classification panel review to confirm eligibility.

One rule worth flagging: every team must include both male and female players, and a woman must be on the ice for every shot. The mixed-gender requirement was firmly in place by PyeongChang 2018. The newest change is mixed doubles at Milano Cortina 2026, played by two athletes per side.

## Glossary
- **End**: A segment in which both teams throw all their stones. A wheelchair game is eight ends.
- **Hammer**: The right to throw the last stone of an end, a major advantage.
- **House**: The concentric rings at each end of the sheet where stones must rest to score.
- **Button**: The center ring of the house. Closest to the button wins the end.
- **Guard**: A stone placed in front of the house to protect a scoring stone behind it.
- **Draw**: A stone thrown with weight to stop inside the house.
- **Takeout**: A stone thrown with extra weight to remove an opponent's stone.
- **Hit**: Casual term for a takeout.
- **Biter**: A stone just barely touching the outer edge of the house.
- **Delivery stick**: The implement that clips to the stone handle so a seated curler can push the stone. Also called an extender.
- **Extender**: Same as delivery stick.
- **Throwing arm**: General term for the arm motion or assistive device used to release a stone.
- **Skip**: The captain who calls strategy and usually throws the last two stones.
- **In-turn / Out-turn**: The two rotation directions applied at release.
- **Blank end**: An end in which no team scores, preserving the hammer.
- **Hack**: The foothold at the back of the sheet. The wheelchair is positioned here but not pushed off.
- **Hog line**: The line a stone must cross to be in play.

## Notable Records & Historical Context
Wheelchair curling debuted at the Torino 2006 Paralympic Winter Games. Canada, skipped by Chris Daw, went undefeated and beat Great Britain in the final to capture the sport's first Paralympic gold.

Canada and China have dominated since. Canada won the first three Paralympic titles (2006, 2010, 2014), then China took gold at PyeongChang 2018 and Beijing 2022. At Milano Cortina 2026, Canada returned to the top of the podium with a perfect campaign, defeating China in the gold medal final for its fourth wheelchair curling gold and first since Sochi 2014. Canada has medaled at every Games since the sport was introduced.

Milano Cortina 2026 also debuted wheelchair curling mixed doubles. China's Jinqiao Yang and Meng Wang won the inaugural gold 9 to 7 over Korea's Hyejin Baek and Yongsuk Lee in an extra end. Latvia took bronze 11 to 10 over the United States, the country's first ever Winter Paralympic medal.

## What to Look For
Watch the delivery first. The thrower sits in a stationary wheelchair, often with a teammate bracing the chair from behind, and pushes the stone with a long extender stick that clips to the handle. The motion is slow and deliberate, more like a pool stroke than the explosive lunge of Olympic curling. Hand release is allowed too.

Because no one is sweeping, weight control is everything. Every stone has to leave the stick at exactly the right speed. That changes the strategic feel: fewer stones in play, more careful draws to the rings, and skips calling safer shots than in the Olympic game. A perfectly weighted draw to the button is the signature shot to admire.

Visual cues for shot type are easy to learn. A broom held deep in the house signals a draw. A broom held outside the house signals a takeout. Watch where the skip rests the broom, that tells you the intent before the stone moves.
